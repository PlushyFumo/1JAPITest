Evolution of media

Select an age to read about its key forms of media.

Before about 3500 BCE

Pre-historic age

Before writing existed, people communicated face to face. Knowledge lived in memory and was passed down by word of mouth.

  • Speech and gestures: the first and most basic forms of communication.
  • Oral tradition: stories, songs, myths and laws memorized and retold across generations.
  • Cuneiform: an early writing system developed in Sumer.
  • Hieroglyphics: an early writing system used in ancient Egypt.
  • Cave paintings and petroglyphs: images on rock walls, such as those at Lascaux in France, recorded hunts, animals and rituals.
  • Signals: drums, smoke and fire carried simple messages over distance.
About 3500 BCE – 1700s

Pre-industrial age

People invented writing systems and materials to store information, so messages could outlast the speaker.

  • Writing: cuneiform in Sumer and hieroglyphs in Egypt, written on clay tablets and papyrus.
  • Alphabets: sound-based scripts spread. In the Philippines, people used Baybayin before Spanish colonization.
  • Paper: developed in China, traditionally credited to Cai Lun around 105 CE.
  • Printing: woodblock printing in Asia, then Johannes Gutenberg’s movable-type press in Europe around 1440, which made books affordable and spread knowledge widely.
1700s – early 1900s

Industrial age

Machines and steam power made it possible to produce and send information quickly and in large amounts. Mass media was born.

  • Newspapers: steam-powered presses made daily papers cheap for the public.
  • Telegraph (1830s–40s): Samuel Morse’s system sent messages by electric signal over wires.
  • Photography (1839): images could be captured and reproduced.
  • Telephone (1876): Alexander Graham Bell enabled voice communication over distance.
  • Typewriter, phonograph and film: sound recording and motion pictures appeared in the late 1800s.
Early 1900s – 1980s

Electronic age

Electricity and electronics carried sound and images to huge audiences at once, in real time.

  • Radio: broadcasting reached homes from the 1920s.
  • Television: became a household medium in the 1950s, adding moving pictures to broadcasts.
  • Transistors and satellites: smaller electronics and satellites such as Telstar (1962) enabled global live transmission.
  • Early computers: from room-sized machines to personal computers in the late 1970s and 1980s.
  • ARPANET (1969): the early network that led to the Internet.
1990s – present

Information age

Digital technology and the Internet make information instant, global and interactive. Audiences can now create and share media themselves.

  • World Wide Web: invented by Tim Berners-Lee, opening the Internet to everyone.
  • Email and search engines: fast communication and access to a huge amount of information.
  • Mobile phones and smartphones: media in your pocket, especially after 2007.
  • Social media and streaming: platforms like Facebook, YouTube and TikTok turned audiences into creators.
  • Artificial intelligence: tools that generate text, images and video, making MIL skills more important than ever.

Tim Berners-Lee

TBL

Sir Tim Berners-Lee

British computer scientist, born in London in 1955, known as the inventor of the World Wide Web.

  • 1989: while working at CERN, the European physics laboratory, he proposed a system for sharing linked documents among researchers.
  • 1990–1991: he built the first web browser and server, and the first website went public in 1991.
  • Core technologies: HTML (page language), HTTP (how pages are transferred) and URL (page addresses).
  • 1994: he founded the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) to develop open web standards.
  • Open web: he chose not to patent the Web, so anyone could use it freely. He later founded the World Wide Web Foundation and was knighted in 2004.

Don’t confuse them: the Internet is the global network of connected computers. The World Wide Web is a service that runs on it, made of linked pages you open in a browser.

Traditional and new media

Traditional media are established channels such as newspapers, magazines, radio, television, books and billboards. New media are digital forms delivered through the Internet and devices, such as websites, blogs, podcasts, social media, streaming and mobile apps.

FeatureTraditional mediaNew media
CommunicationMostly one-way: audience receivesTwo-way: audience can comment, share and respond
SpeedSlower, fixed schedules (daily paper, evening news)Instant and always available
Who publishesProfessional organizations with editors as gatekeepersAnyone with a device and connection
ReachLimited by distribution, signal or print runGlobal, across borders
EditingHard to change once printed or airedEasy to update, edit or delete
FormatUsually one format (print, audio or video)Multimedia: text, images, audio, video together
Cost to audienceOften paid (newspapers, cable)Often free, supported by ads or subscriptions
ReliabilityUsually fact-checked, but can be biasedVaries widely; misinformation spreads easily
ExamplesNewspaper, radio, TV, magazineWebsites, Facebook, YouTube, podcasts, blogs

Convergence: the two are blending. Newspapers have websites and TV networks stream online, so MIL skills apply to every kind of media.
