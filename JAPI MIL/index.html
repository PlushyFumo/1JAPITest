Media and Information Literacy

Every day we receive thousands of messages. MIL helps you decide which to trust, how to use them, and how to create and share your own responsibly.

What is MIL?

Media and Information Literacy (MIL) is the set of competencies that lets people access, retrieve, understand, evaluate, use, create and share information and media content in all formats, using different tools, in a critical, ethical and effective way. UNESCO promotes it as a basic skill for citizens in the digital age.

Key idea: MIL is not just about using gadgets. It is about thinking critically about what you see, read and hear, and acting responsibly on it.

Why do we need it?

  • Information overload: there is more content than anyone can read.
  • Fake news, misleading edits and deepfakes spread fast.
  • Anyone can now publish, so everyone is both audience and author.
  • Informed citizens make better decisions about health, school, work and voting.

Communication and media literacy

The communication process

Communication is the sharing of meaning between people. Every message travels through the same basic parts:

Sender›Message›Channel›Receiver›Feedback
  • Sender: creates and encodes the message.
  • Message: the idea, information or feeling being shared.
  • Channel: the medium used, such as speech, print, radio, TV or the internet.
  • Receiver: decodes and interprets the message.
  • Feedback: the receiver’s response, which tells the sender if the message was understood.
  • Noise: anything that disturbs the message, such as loud sounds, poor signal or bias.

The three types of literacy

MIL brings together three related literacies. A competent MIL user needs all of them.

Media literacy

Understanding the role and functions of media, and evaluating media content and its makers.

Example: noticing bias or sensational wording in a news report.

Information literacy

Recognizing when information is needed, then locating, evaluating, using and citing it.

Example: choosing a credible journal article over an anonymous blog for research.

Technology literacy

Using tools, devices, software and platforms effectively, safely and responsibly.

Example: using search operators, cloud storage and privacy settings.

Remember: Media literacy asks “what is this message?”, information literacy asks “is it reliable and how do I use it?”, and technology literacy asks “which tool helps me do this safely?”

Supporting digital literacy

Digital literacy is the ability to find, evaluate, create and communicate information using digital technologies. You can build it, and help others build it, through everyday habits.

Check before you believe: SIFT

  • Stop: pause before reacting or sharing.
  • Investigate the source: who is behind it, and what is their expertise?
  • Find better coverage: see what other reliable outlets say.
  • Trace claims: go back to the original quote, study or photo.

Other ways to support digital literacy

  • Protect your privacy with strong passwords, two-factor authentication and careful privacy settings.
  • Learn new tools and keep your skills updated.
  • Create content, not only consume it. Making a video or blog teaches how media is built.
  • Practice good digital citizenship: be respectful, inclusive and kind online.
  • Teach family and classmates, especially about scams and false information.

Responsible use of information

Having access to information comes with responsibility. Responsible users respect the rights of creators, the privacy of others and the truth.

Do

  • Verify facts before sharing.
  • Cite and credit your sources.
  • Ask permission or use properly licensed content.
  • Protect personal data, yours and others’.
  • Follow netiquette: be polite and respectful.

Don’t

  • Plagiarize or pass off others’ work as yours.
  • Spread rumors, hoaxes or edited photos as fact.
  • Post private information or harass others.
  • Download pirated materials.
  • Share clickbait without reading it.

Laws and ideas to know

  • Copyright: legal protection of creative work. In the Philippines this falls under the Intellectual Property Code (RA 8293).
  • Fair use: limited use of copyrighted work, such as quoting for study or criticism, with proper credit.
  • Data privacy: the Data Privacy Act (RA 10173) protects personal information.
  • Cybercrime: the Cybercrime Prevention Act (RA 10175) covers offenses such as online libel and identity theft.
